## Short answer

Not in the way most people hope. There is no good evidence that a stem cell injection regrows a worn-out
knee's cartilage. What the injection research does show is more modest: a 2025 Cochrane review found that
injections may slightly improve knee pain and function for up to six months, compared with a placebo
injection. None of the trials it included checked whether the joint itself changed on an X-ray.

Cartilage regrowth has been shown in a different setting: stem cells *implanted surgically* into a
single, well-defined hole in the cartilage. That is an operation, not an injection, and it is aimed at a
different kind of knee.

These therapies are considered investigational and are not approved by the U.S. FDA or Health Canada
for knee osteoarthritis. Results vary from person to person, and some people notice no improvement.

## "Cartilage damage" means two different things

Cartilage is the smooth layer that lets the ends of the bones glide. It has no blood supply, which is
why it barely repairs itself. But a "cartilage problem" can mean two very different knees, and the
answer depends on which one you have.

| Your knee | What it usually looks like | What has been studied |
|---|---|---|
| **A focal defect** | One hole or crater in otherwise healthy cartilage, often after an injury | Surgical repair: microfracture, cultured cartilage cells, and — in trials — surgically implanted stem cells |
| **Osteoarthritis (wear)** | Cartilage thinning across a whole area of the joint, usually with age | Injections into the joint, to ease pain and inflammation |
| **Bone on bone** | Little or no cartilage left; the joint space has collapsed on an X-ray | Usually the conversation about knee replacement. Injections have the least to work with here |

Most people searching for "cartilage repair" in their 50s, 60s and 70s have the second kind. The
research that shows new cartilage is mostly about the first.

## What the injection studies actually measured

- **Pain and function: a small benefit.** A 2025 Cochrane review — the most rigorous kind of evidence
  summary — gathered 25 randomised trials with 1,341 people. In the seven that compared stem cells with
  a placebo injection (445 people), pain improved by about 1.2 points on a 0-to-10 scale, up to six
  months out. The reviewers rated that
  evidence low certainty: the cell source, preparation and dose varied a great deal between trials,
  and up to three larger trials were withdrawn before reporting results.
- **Cartilage: not measured.** None of the 25 trials assessed whether the joint changed on an X-ray.
  The reviewers say plainly that the effect on structure remains uncertain.
- **MRI: a mixed picture.** An earlier review of 10 smaller trials found more cartilage volume on MRI
  in the stem cell groups, but no difference on a standard MRI score of overall joint damage. It also
  counted more adverse events in the stem cell groups than in the controls.

Part of what people feel after any knee injection comes from expectation and the procedure itself. We
cover that research in our post on [stem cell therapy for arthritis and joint pain](/en/blog/stem-cell-therapy-arthritis-joint-pain/).

Researchers now think the cells mainly work by releasing signals that calm inflammation, rather than by
turning into new cartilage. That fits the results: a possible improvement in how the knee feels, with
little proof that it rebuilds what has worn away.

## Where new cartilage has been shown: surgery, not an injection

- **Implanted stem cells.** A randomised trial in South Korea treated 114 people, average age 56, who
  had a large, full-thickness cartilage defect. Through a small open incision, surgeons either implanted
  umbilical cord blood stem cells in a gel or performed microfracture. A year later, a camera inside the
  joint showed better repair tissue in the stem cell group. Pain and function were similar at one year,
  and better in the stem cell group at three to five years.
- **Cultured cartilage cells.** In the U.S., the FDA approved MACI in 2016 for full-thickness defects
  in adults. It uses the patient's own cartilage cells, grown in a laboratory, not stem cells, and it
  takes two surgeries.
- **Microfracture.** The surgeon makes small holes in the bone under the defect so marrow fills the gap.
  What grows back tends to be fibrocartilage, a tougher but less smooth tissue than the original.

All three are operations for a defined hole in the cartilage. None is a substitute for a knee
replacement in a joint that has worn through.

Before your evaluation, gather:

- **A standing X-ray** of the knee, which shows how much joint space is left.
- **An MRI**, if you have one, which shows the cartilage and any single defect.
- **What you have already tried**: physical therapy, weight loss, other injections, and how each went.

And ask the treating physician:

- Which kind of cartilage problem does my imaging show?
- What would count as a good result for my knee, and by when?

## FAQ

### Can stem cells regrow cartilage in a bone-on-bone knee?
There is no good evidence that they can. Where the cartilage has worn through, an injection has very
little tissue to work with. A physician who has seen your X-ray can tell you whether there is anything
left to treat, or whether knee replacement is the more honest conversation.

### Will an MRI show new cartilage after an injection?
Possibly not. Some small trials reported more cartilage volume on MRI; others found no difference, and
the large Cochrane review found no trial that checked structure on X-ray. Judge the result mainly by
pain and function, and agree with your physician in advance on how to measure them.

### Can a stem cell injection help me avoid a knee replacement?
No trial has shown that. Some people use injections to manage symptoms while they put off surgery, but
whether that delays a replacement has not been established. Be wary of any provider who promises it.

### How long until I know whether it worked?
Most patient reports place any change between the first few weeks and about six months. See
[how long results take to appear](/en/blog/stem-cell-therapy-results-timeline/) and
[how long the effects last](/en/blog/how-long-does-stem-cell-therapy-last/).

### Is it approved by the FDA?
No. Stem cell injections for knee osteoarthritis are not approved by the FDA or Health Canada and are
considered investigational. MACI, the surgical cartilage-cell product, is FDA-approved, but only for
focal defects and it does not use stem cells.
