## Short answer

Yes — when it's performed by a licensed physician, using properly sourced cells, in an accredited lab. Most of the real danger doesn't come from the therapy itself; it comes from unlicensed clinics cutting corners on sourcing, sterility, or oversight. Knowing the difference is the single best thing you can do to protect yourself.

## What "stem cell therapy" actually means here

One thing worth clearing up first: a lot of search results for "stem cell transplant" are actually about bone marrow (hematopoietic) transplants used to treat blood cancers — a completely different procedure, with a different risk profile involving chemotherapy and extended hospital stays. That's not what regenerative medicine clinics like the ones in our network offer. This article is about **mesenchymal stem cell (MSC) therapy** — the kind used for joint, orthopedic, and wellness applications. [See how it works →](/en/services/stem-cells/)

## The real risks

**Common and usually mild**
- Injection-site pain, swelling, or bruising. Research on MSC injections suggests this affects a meaningful share of patients, typically resolving within 2–7 days.
- Mild fatigue or a low-grade fever for a day or two.

**Less common but more serious**
- Infection, if sterile technique or lab standards are poor.
- An immune reaction — more likely if donor (allogeneic) cells are used rather than your own.
- Rare reports of unwanted tissue growth when cell sourcing or processing isn't properly controlled.

None of these risks disappear entirely with a good provider — but they become far less likely.

## Why some clinics are riskier than others

Regulators and patient-safety organizations have repeatedly flagged "stem cell tourism" — patients traveling for treatment at clinics with weak oversight — as the common thread behind the most serious complications. The therapy itself isn't inherently the danger; **who's providing it, and how,** is.

## How to tell a legitimate clinic from a risky one

| Ask this | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Is the clinic and lab government-licensed? (In Mexico, that's COFEPRIS.) | Confirms basic regulatory oversight of the facility. |
| Can they show a Certificate of Analysis for the cell batch? | Documents cell source, count, viability, and sterility testing. |
| Do you get a physical evaluation before treatment — not just a phone call? | A real evaluation is how a physician catches when a therapy isn't right for you. |
| Who is actually treating you? | Ask for the treating physician's name and credentials, not just the coordinator's. |

## Frequently asked questions

### Is it normal to feel sore after treatment?
Mild soreness, swelling, or bruising at the injection site is common and usually resolves within a few days. Significant pain, fever, or swelling that gets worse — not better — after 48 hours isn't typical and should be reported to your physician right away.

### Can I ask to see the cell Certificate of Analysis before treatment?
Yes, and you should. A legitimate laboratory will have no problem showing you documentation of the cell source, count, viability, and sterility testing before your procedure.

### Does "government-licensed" mean the treatment is FDA-approved?
No — those are different things. A government license (like COFEPRIS in Mexico) means the facility and providers meet that country's regulatory requirements. Many regenerative therapies are not FDA- or Health Canada-approved for specific conditions and are still considered investigational; ask your provider directly about the current evidence for your case.
