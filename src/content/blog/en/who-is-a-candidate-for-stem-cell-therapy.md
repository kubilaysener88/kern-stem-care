## Short answer

You are a candidate for stem cell therapy when a physician, having reviewed your history and your test
results, confirms three things: that your problem is one these cells have been studied for, that you have
no condition that would make it unsafe — such as cancer in the past 5 years, an active infection or a
pregnancy — and that what you expect from it is realistic.

"Anyone who wants a better quality of life" is not a medical criterion. If a provider says yes before
looking at your records, you were not evaluated. You were sold to.

These therapies are considered investigational and are not approved by the U.S. FDA or Health Canada for
specific diseases. Results vary from person to person.

## What the physician checks before saying yes

The decision belongs to the treating physician, not a web form or whoever answers the WhatsApp. In
practice, they look at three things.

**1. What you have, and whether it has been studied.** The mesenchymal stem cells we coordinate — the kind
used in regenerative medicine, not the bone-marrow transplant used for blood cancers — are used mostly for
joints, orthopedic injuries and general wellness, and those uses do not all carry the same evidence.
Severity matters too. A registered phase III
trial in Shanghai, injecting umbilical-cord cells into the knee, only admits Kellgren-Lawrence grade II to
III osteoarthritis: mild to moderate wear. A bone-on-bone knee has very little left to work with, which we
explain in [can stem cells repair knee cartilage?](/en/blog/can-stem-cells-repair-knee-cartilage/).

**2. Your general health.** Most of the trials' exclusions sit here. The table below sets them out.

**3. Your expectations.** If you expect a cure, a rebuilt joint or a way to avoid a knee replacement, the
evidence does not support that. A good candidate is looking for a possible improvement in pain and function, and
accepts that they may notice none.

## Who usually isn't a candidate

Clinical trials publish who they exclude, and that list is the best public guide there is. We took two:
the Shanghai knee trial, and CRATUS, a U.S. trial that gave donor cells by IV to older adults with
frailty. They agree on almost everything that matters.

| Situation | What the trials required | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| **Cancer** | No cancer in the past 5 years, in both trials (CRATUS allowed some treated skin cancers) | Nobody knows for certain how these cells interact with a tumour |
| **Pregnancy or breastfeeding** | Excluded in both | There is no safety data for mother or baby |
| **Active infection** | Excluded in the knee trial, as was a skin infection where the injection goes | An injection over an infection can spread it |
| **Clotting problems** | INR above 1.5 excluded in both. CRATUS made an exception when it was caused by a blood thinner such as warfarin | Higher bleeding risk; if you take blood thinners, the physician has to know |
| **Serious heart, liver or kidney disease** | Excluded in both | The body has less margin to cope with any reaction |
| **HIV, hepatitis B or C** | Excluded in both | The trials checked for it with blood tests before treating |
| **Recent cortisone shot in the joint** | The knee trial required 12 weeks without one | It muddies the result: you would not know what helped |

Two caveats. First, a trial excludes some people to protect their health and others only to keep the
result clean: the knee trial also excluded rheumatoid arthritis and gout, because it studied
osteoarthritis alone. That alone does not make them a no for you: the physician weighs it. Second, a clinic is not bound by a
trial's list. But a careful physician asks about everything in that table, and if nobody asks you, that
tells you something too.

## What doesn't rule you out on its own

- **Age.** The knee trial admitted people from 40 to 80, and CRATUS from 60 to 95. What weighs is your
  health, not the number.
- **Having tried other treatments without success.** Quite the opposite: the knee trial required pain
  lasting more than six months, or osteoarthritis that came back once the usual treatment stopped.
- **Having more than one painful joint.** Each is considered separately, and the physician decides which
  make sense.

One separate case: if you are looking for treatment for leukaemia, lymphoma or another blood cancer, what
you need is a bone-marrow transplant. That is a different procedure, done in a transplant hospital with
your haematologist. We explain the difference in
[what is a cell therapy center?](/en/blog/what-is-a-cell-therapy-center/).

## FAQ

### Is there an age limit for stem cell therapy?
There is no fixed number. The trials we reviewed accepted people up to 80 and up to 95. What decides it is
your overall health and what your test results show.

### Can I have stem cell therapy if I have had cancer?
Both trials we reviewed excluded anyone with cancer in the past 5 years. If yours was longer ago, tell the
physician and bring your follow-up results: it is a case-by-case decision, and your oncologist should know
about it too.

### Can I have it if I take blood thinners?
Tell the physician before the evaluation. One of the trials excluded people with abnormal clotting, though
it made an exception when a blood thinner was the cause. If a joint is going to be injected, the physician
decides whether anything needs adjusting; do not stop any medication on your own.

### Am I a candidate if I have diabetes?
Neither trial excluded diabetes as such; both excluded serious heart, liver or kidney disease, which
sometimes comes with it. Bring recent test results to the evaluation and the physician will weigh it
with you.

### What happens if the physician says no?
It ends there, at no cost. A timely no is the most useful thing an evaluation can give you: it saves you a
trip, a payment and a false hope. If you want a second opinion, take the physician's explanation to your
own doctor.
