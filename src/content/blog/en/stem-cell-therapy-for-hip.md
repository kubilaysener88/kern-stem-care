## Short answer

Stem cell therapy for the hip is studied mainly in hip arthritis, and the evidence is still early: a 2025
review found 9 clinical studies, none of them randomised, in which pain fell by an average of 30% to 50%
after the injection. No major complications were reported, and the side effects described were mild —
mostly temporary discomfort and swelling in the joint.

What those studies do not clearly show is cartilage growing back: imaging results were few and
inconsistent. And without randomised trials, the effect of the cells cannot be fully separated from the
placebo effect or from pain easing on its own.

These therapies are considered investigational and are not approved by the U.S. FDA or Health Canada for
any hip condition. Results vary from person to person, and some people notice no improvement.

## Which hip problem you have changes the answer

"Hip pain" can come from very different problems, and not all of them are inside the joint.

| Your hip | What it is | What the stem cell research shows |
|---|---|---|
| **Mild to moderate arthritis** | Cartilage wear, with joint space still visible on the X-ray | Small, non-randomised studies: less pain and better function; a 2022 review of injected biologics suggests results are better the less advanced it is |
| **Advanced arthritis** ("bone on bone") | The cartilage is essentially gone | Little evidence; hip replacement remains the established treatment |
| **Labral tear** | Damage to the ring of cartilage around the hip socket | A 2024 review found no overall improvement over controls when bone marrow concentrate was added during surgery |
| **Avascular necrosis** | Part of the femoral head loses its blood supply | A different treatment: cells placed inside the bone during surgery. See [bone regeneration](/en/blog/stem-cell-therapy-for-bone-regeneration/) |

One practical point: pain that starts in the hip joint itself is usually felt in the groin or the front of
the thigh. When it hurts mostly on the outside of the hip, the source is often the tendons or the bursa
there; when it hurts in the buttock, it sometimes comes from the lower back. That is why an evaluation
starts by confirming where the pain comes from, before anyone talks about cells.

## What the studies actually found

- **The reviews.** A 2024 systematic review brought together 10 studies with 316 patients who received
  mesenchymal stem cells into the hip. The studies reported improvements in pain and function, with few
  side effects, but the authors stress that high-quality studies are still limited. An earlier review, from
  2022, counted 20 studies of different biologics for the hip: the only six randomised trials were of
  platelet-rich plasma (PRP), none of cells.
- **A single injection of fat-derived cells.** In Naples, 30 patients with hip arthritis that had not
  improved with anti-inflammatories or physiotherapy received one injection of cells from their own fat.
  In 27 of them, pain, function and quality of life improved and stayed that way through 12 months of
  follow-up. Two went on to have a hip replacement. It was a retrospective study with no comparison
  group.
- **Cultured bone marrow cells.** In Santiago, Chile, 10 patients received three weekly injections of
  cells from their own bone marrow, multiplied in a laboratory. Hip function and range of motion improved, and in
  9 of the 10 the X-ray showed no progression of the arthritis. Not getting worse is not the same as
  cartilage regenerating.

One detail matters when you compare offers: the Naples and Santiago studies used the *patient's own*
cells, from fat or bone marrow. Many providers instead use donor cells grown in a laboratory, often from
umbilical cord tissue. That is a different product, and these results do not automatically carry over to
it. Ask which one you would receive. Our piece on
[what a cell therapy center does](/en/blog/what-is-a-cell-therapy-center/) explains the difference.

## What this means if you are considering treatment

If your X-ray shows advanced arthritis and the pain no longer lets you walk or sleep, the most useful
first step is usually an assessment by an orthopedic surgeon at home. Hip replacement is an established
operation, and there is no evidence that an injection replaces it in a badly worn joint.

For mild to moderate arthritis that has not improved with physiotherapy, weight management and pain
relief, a physician may consider a stem cell injection a reasonable option to try to reduce pain and
improve function. It has not been shown to delay a hip replacement: no trial has tested that.

The hip sits deep beneath several layers of muscle, so an injection into the joint is normally done with
image guidance — ultrasound or X-ray (fluoroscopy) — to make sure the cells end up inside it rather than
in the tissue around it. Ask the clinic which one it uses.

Before your evaluation, gather:

- **A recent X-ray of the pelvis or hip**, which shows how much joint space is left.
- **An MRI**, if you have one, especially if a labral tear or necrosis is suspected.
- **What you have already tried**: physiotherapy, cortisone, hyaluronic acid or PRP injections, and how
  each one went.

And ask the treating physician:

- Is my pain coming from the joint, the tendons around it or my spine?
- What grade is my arthritis? Am I in the range where injections have been studied?
- What would a good result look like for my hip in six months?

## Frequently asked questions

### Do stem cells regenerate hip cartilage?
That has not been shown. The hip studies mostly measured pain and function; imaging was limited and the
results inconsistent. The same is true in the knee, where there is more research: see
[can stem cells repair knee cartilage?](/en/blog/can-stem-cells-repair-knee-cartilage/)

### Can a stem cell injection help me avoid a hip replacement?
No trial has shown that. In the Naples study, two of the 30 patients went on to have a hip replacement.
Be wary of any provider who promises you will avoid the operation.

### Is platelet-rich plasma (PRP) better for the hip?
PRP has more randomised trials in the hip than stem cells do, but its results there are less consistent
than in the knee: two reviews of trials comparing it with hyaluronic acid found no clear difference at
final follow-up.

### Does the hip injection hurt?
Expect soreness at the injection site and, sometimes, a temporary increase in pain or stiffness in the
first few days, which usually settles within one to two weeks.
[Is it normal for pain to get worse before it gets better?](/en/blog/pain-worse-before-better-stem-cell-therapy/)
explains what is expected and what is not.

### How long does the effect last?
Hip studies report short- to mid-term results; in the Naples study, for example, follow-up was 12
months. There is no solid longer-term data. See
[how long stem cell therapy lasts](/en/blog/how-long-does-stem-cell-therapy-last/).

### Is it FDA-approved?
No. Stem cell injections for hip arthritis are not approved by the FDA or Health Canada and are considered
investigational.
