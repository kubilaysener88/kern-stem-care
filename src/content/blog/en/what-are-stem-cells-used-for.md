## Short answer

Stem cells are used to replace cells and help repair tissue, but they have one established use: **blood
stem cell transplants** for leukemia, lymphoma and other blood diseases. In the U.S., nearly every stem cell product the FDA has approved
is made of blood-forming cells, plus **a single** mesenchymal product, for a complication of transplants in
children. None is for joints, skin or anti-aging.

The stem cells that are **injected** into a knee, a shoulder or a vein are a different kind:
*mesenchymal* cells. The research there is real and in places encouraging, but these uses are
investigational and are not approved by the U.S. FDA or Health Canada. In our network, a single-joint
application costs **$2,300 USD**.

So the useful answer to "what are they used for?" depends on which cell and which problem. Each case is
below, with what the studies actually show.

## Three kinds of stem cell, three different answers

When an article says "stem cells can be used for…", it is usually blending cell types that have little
to do with each other.

| Type | Where they come from | What they are used for today |
|---|---|---|
| **Hematopoietic** (blood-forming) | Bone marrow, blood or umbilical cord blood | Transplants for leukemia, lymphoma and other blood diseases. Established treatment, in hospital, after chemotherapy |
| **Mesenchymal** (MSCs) | Bone marrow, fat, umbilical cord | Joint injections, intravenous applications, and research in many conditions. Outside one narrow pediatric use, investigational |
| **Embryonic** | Early-stage embryos | Research, including some early clinical trials. Not given in regenerative clinics |

The cells we coordinate are **[mesenchymal](/en/services/stem-cells/)**. A bone marrow transplant is a
different procedure, done at a cancer or hematology hospital, not a regenerative clinic.

## What is approved

The FDA puts it plainly: the only stem cell products approved in the U.S. were blood-forming cells from
**umbilical cord blood**, for disorders that affect the production of blood — and it adds that they are
not approved for other uses. Since then, a few gene therapies have been approved that also start from
blood stem cells — the patient's own, corrected in a laboratory — for rare inherited diseases.

In December 2024 the first mesenchymal product joined them: **Ryoncil**, donor bone-marrow cells,
approved for children 2 months and older with steroid-refractory acute *graft-versus-host disease* — a
serious complication of the bone marrow transplant itself. The approval rested on a study of 54 children
with no comparison group.

Beyond that, no approval covers a knee injection, a wellness IV or a facial treatment. It is worth
knowing, because plenty of pages use "approved" as if it covered every stem cell application. It does
not.

## What injected stem cells are used for

These are the uses people ask about most, and what the research shows for each. All of them are
investigational.

| Area | What the research shows | More detail |
|---|---|---|
| **Knee osteoarthritis** | A 2025 Cochrane review found injections may slightly improve pain and function for up to six months compared with placebo. There is no good evidence they regrow cartilage | [Knee cartilage](/en/blog/can-stem-cells-repair-knee-cartilage/) |
| **Shoulder and rotator cuff** | The strongest evidence is for cells added *during* surgery: fewer re-tears. For an injection on its own, small studies | [Shoulder](/en/blog/stem-cell-therapy-for-shoulder-rotator-cuff/) |
| **Spine and discs** | A modest benefit at best. The largest trial, with 404 patients, missed its primary goal | [Herniated disc](/en/blog/stem-cell-therapy-for-back-pain-herniated-disc/) |
| **Bone** | Fractures that will not heal and early hip necrosis: encouraging but limited, and almost always with cells placed during surgery | [Bone regeneration](/en/blog/stem-cell-therapy-for-bone-regeneration/) |
| **General wellness by IV** | Safety studied in controlled trials; evidence of benefit as anti-aging is limited | [IV or joint](/en/blog/stem-cell-iv-vs-joint-injection/) |

The pattern across the table: when improvement shows up, it is usually in **pain and function**, it
varies a lot from person to person, and some people notice no change. That is why a physician reviews
your case before recommending anything.

## What stem cells cannot do

- **They do not come as a pill or a cream.** Those products do not contain living human stem cells. We
  explain why in [stem cell pills, capsules and creams](/en/blog/stem-cell-pills-capsules-and-creams/).
- **They are not "for everything".** A clinic advertising the same injection for arthritis, autism,
  diabetes and Parkinson's is selling a list, not evidence.
- **They do not replace the care you already have.** A knee injection does not stand in for your own
  doctor or your medication.

## FAQ

### What diseases can stem cells cure?
Blood stem cell transplants are used for leukemia, lymphoma and other blood diseases, and in some of them
they can lead to lasting remission. That is a hospital treatment with chemotherapy, not an injection. For
the mesenchymal cells given into joints or by IV, there is no evidence that they cure any disease: what is
being studied is whether they ease symptoms such as pain.

### What are stem cell injections used for?
Injected into a joint, they are studied mainly for pain and function in osteoarthritis of the knee,
shoulder or hip. The strongest evidence, for the knee, shows slight improvements for up to six months
compared with placebo. It is an investigational use, and results vary from person to person.

### Are stem cells used on the face?
In our network, the facial application does not use stem cells: it uses **fibroblasts**, the cells that
make collagen in the skin. See [fibroblast therapy for the skin](/en/blog/fibroblast-therapy-for-skin/).

### How long do the effects of stem cells last?
Nobody can honestly give you a number of years. Most knee trials report short-term results and few follow
patients longer; one small trial still saw improvement four years after a single injection. We go through
it in [how long stem cell therapy lasts](/en/blog/how-long-does-stem-cell-therapy-last/).
