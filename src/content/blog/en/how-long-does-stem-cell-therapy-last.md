## Short answer

Nobody can honestly give you a number of years. Most clinical trials of stem cell injections for knee
arthritis followed patients for 6 to 24 months. A handful went further. One small trial still saw
improvement four years after a single injection, and a European review found benefits at two to three
years — while warning that the evidence is limited. Search around and you will see confident figures
like "two to five years" or "up to fifteen." We could not find controlled studies that support numbers
that precise.

You can have a second treatment. The one randomized trial that compared one dose with two in the knee
found the clearest benefit at one year in the group that got two. It was a small study, so it suggests
an answer rather than settling one.

These therapies are considered investigational and are not approved by the U.S. FDA or Health Canada for
these uses. Results vary from person to person, and some people notice no improvement at all.

## The cells leave long before the effect does

This surprises most people. Mesenchymal stem cells do not move in and stay. In a 2012 study in mice, cells
given through a vein were found alive in the lungs for up to 24 hours. After that they were gone. The
authors concluded that any longer-lasting effect has to work through the body's own cells.

That fits what researchers now think these cells mostly do: they release signals that calm inflammation
and support the tissue's own repair. The signal is brief, and the body's response to it can last much
longer. So "how long does it last?" is really a question about how long your joint holds on to that
response — and that depends on the joint as much as on the cells.

## What the studies actually measured

| Study | What was given | How long patients were followed | What it found |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lamo-Espinosa et al., Spain, 2018 — 30 patients | One injection of the patient's own bone marrow cells into the knee | 4 years | Pain and function were still better than at the start. The control group's pain had worsened |
| ESSKA review, 2023 — 9 studies | Stem cell implantation for knee arthritis | 2–3 years | Better pain and function scores, but few studies had a control group or mid-term results |
| Matas et al., Chile, 2019 — 26 patients | Umbilical cord cells: one dose, two doses six months apart, or hyaluronic acid | 1 year | The two-dose group had the lowest pain at one year. No difference showed on MRI |
| Fares et al., review of 43 trials, 2024 | Stem cell injections for arthritis, mostly knee | Varied | Most trials reported improvement, but protocols differed so much that the authors called the results equivocal |

Two things stand out. The groups are small — dozens of patients, not thousands. And almost all of the
long follow-up is in the knee. For other joints, and for IV infusions given for general wellness, there is
far less data on how long anything lasts.

## Why it varies so much from one person to the next

- **How worn the joint is.** The ESSKA review found the benefit in mild to moderate arthritis. A joint
  that is bone on bone has less tissue left to respond.
- **What the cells were and how many.** Source, dose and lab preparation differ between protocols. That
  is one reason the 2024 review struggled to compare trials.
- **Where they went.** A joint injection keeps the cells in the joint. An IV infusion sends most of them
  to the lungs first. We explain the difference in [IV vs. joint injection](/en/blog/stem-cell-iv-vs-joint-injection/).
- **What happens next.** Weight on the joint, activity, and following the physical therapy plan all keep
  working on the joint after the treatment.

## If you are thinking about a second treatment

A repeat treatment is a medical decision. It belongs to the treating physician, after a fresh look at how
you responded. A few points worth knowing before that conversation:

- **Timing.** In the Chilean trial, the second dose came six months after the first, and it was planned
  from the start. It was not given because the first dose had "worn off."
- **Safety.** No serious adverse events were reported in that trial's two-dose group. Long-term data on
  repeated doses is still thin.
- **Cost.** A second application is a separate quote. In our network, one joint is $2,300 USD — see the
  [price list](/en/pricing/). Follow-up at home and any later applications sit outside the first quote,
  and we say so in writing before you travel.
- **Whether it worked the first time.** If you noticed nothing after six months, ask the physician why
  before you pay for a second round. Repeating a treatment that did nothing for you is not automatically
  the next step.

## FAQ

### Is stem cell therapy permanent?
No study shows a permanent effect. The cells do not settle in for good — in the tracking study above,
IV cells were gone within a day — and the effects measured in trials are followed for months to a few
years. Think of it as a treatment
whose effect may last a while, not a one-time fix.

### How long does a stem cell knee injection last?
In the published trials, improvement was usually measured at one to two years. One small study still saw
it at four years. How long it lasts for you depends on how worn the joint is and on your own biology. A
physician who has seen your imaging can give you a far better estimate than any general figure.

### How many times can you have stem cell therapy?
There is no set limit, and also no evidence-based schedule. Repeat dosing has been studied in small trials,
mostly in the knee. How often makes sense for you is for your physician to decide.

### Do exosome, fibroblast and placenta treatments last as long?
