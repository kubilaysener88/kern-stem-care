## Short answer

**[Mesenchymal stem cell (MSC) therapy](/en/services/stem-cells)** for knee and joint arthritis is one of the
more extensively studied applications of cellular therapy. Multiple systematic reviews and meta-analyses of
clinical trials report meaningful improvements in pain and physical function after intra-articular MSC
injection — but researchers are also clear that some of that improvement may come from placebo and other
contextual effects, and that long-term safety and effectiveness data from large trials is still limited.
It is not a guaranteed cure, and it is not right for every case — a licensed physician evaluates that.

## What the research actually shows

- **Pain and function.** Reviews of randomized controlled trials report significant improvements in pain
  scores and standard function measures (such as WOMAC) after MSC injection into the knee, in some cases
  sustained through 12-month follow-up.
- **How it's thought to work.** MSCs are studied for their ability to modulate local inflammation, support
  the joint's microenvironment, and encourage tissue repair processes — not for regrowing new cartilage from
  scratch.
- **An honest caveat.** A 2025 meta-analysis specifically looking at *contextual effects* — the improvement
  patients experience from expectation, attention, and the injection procedure itself, independent of the
  cells — found that a substantial share of the reported benefit may come from these factors rather than the
  MSCs alone. This doesn't mean MSC therapy doesn't work; it means the size of the specific cellular effect
  is still being refined by ongoing research.
- **Safety profile.** Reported adverse events are generally mild — pain, swelling, or stiffness at the
  injection site (see our guide on [why pain can flare before it improves](/en/blog/pain-worse-before-better-stem-cell-therapy)).
  Reviewers consistently note that long-term data from larger trials is still needed.

## Common questions

### Does stem cell therapy regrow cartilage?
Current evidence points to MSCs modulating inflammation and supporting the joint environment more than
regenerating new cartilage tissue outright. Ask your physician what mechanism is expected to help your
specific case.

### How much of the improvement is placebo?
Research is actively studying this. A 2025 meta-analysis found that contextual effects (expectation,
attention, the injection process) account for a meaningful share of reported improvement — which is one
reason realistic expectations, set by a licensed physician, matter more than marketing claims.

### Is this approved by the FDA?
No — like other cellular therapies, MSC treatment for arthritis is not approved by the U.S. FDA or Health
Canada for specific conditions and is considered investigational. See our guide on
[what "investigational" means and how Mexico regulates this](/en/blog/is-stem-cell-therapy-legal-in-mexico).

### How does this compare to exosome therapy for joints?
Both are studied for joint-related concerns, but they're different biological approaches. See our
[stem cells vs. exosomes comparison](/en/blog/stem-cells-vs-exosomes-difference) for the distinction.
