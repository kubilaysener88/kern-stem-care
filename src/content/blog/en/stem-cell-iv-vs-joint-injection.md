## Short answer

The route decides where the cells go. An **injection into the joint** puts the cells where the problem
is — a knee, a hip, a shoulder — and it is the route used in most of the joint research. An **IV
infusion** sends the cells into the bloodstream, where — in animal studies — most of them first lodge in the lungs; it is used
for whole-body goals such as general wellness, and the evidence for those goals is thinner.

So the useful question is not "which is better?" but "which one matches what I want treated?" For one
painful joint, the research points to local injection. For a general wellness goal, IV is the route
used — and it is worth going in with realistic expectations.

These therapies are considered investigational and are not approved by the U.S. FDA or Health Canada for
these uses. The treating physician recommends the route for your case after reviewing your history and
imaging.

## Where IV cells actually go

This is the part most IV marketing leaves out. Mesenchymal stem cells are relatively large. When they are
infused into a vein, the first organ they pass through is the lungs, and the lungs' tiny capillaries catch
most of them. A 2009 study from the University of Texas — in rats — traced the cells and found **the
majority trapped in the lungs** after the first pass, with relatively few reaching the arterial
circulation.

That does not mean IV cells do nothing. Researchers think much of what these cells do comes from the
signals they release — molecules that calm inflammation and support repair — rather than from the cells
settling in a specific spot. Signals can travel where cells cannot. But it does mean an IV drip is not a
way to "send" cells to your knee. If a joint is the problem, the joint is where the research puts them.

A small animal study compared both routes directly in arthritic knees: the joints that received the
cells by injection showed better tissue healing than those that received them intravenously. It was a
study in rats, so it points the way rather than settling the question.

## What the safety research shows for IV

The best safety data for IV mesenchymal stem cells comes from a research group in Ottawa that has pooled
randomized trials for years:

- A 2020 review of **55 randomized trials (2,696 patients)** found that the cells were linked to more
  frequent **fever** after the infusion — and no higher rate of infection, blood clots, death or cancer
  compared with control groups.
- A 2026 update focused on umbilical-cord cells, **42 trials (2,280 patients)**, found the same pattern:
  more fever, no other safety signal, and no trial stopped early for safety.

Two honest caveats. These were controlled trials, run with laboratory-tested cells and careful
monitoring — the safety record belongs to that way of working, not to IV therapy anywhere. And the
authors conclude the cells appear safe *to keep studying*, which is different from proven to work. That
is why who prepares the cells matters as much as the route: the laboratory that prepares them for our
network, NAO Biotechnology, ships every batch with a signed Certificate of Quality.

For joint injections, the usual effects are local: soreness, swelling or stiffness for a few days. Our
article on [pain getting worse before it gets better](/en/blog/pain-worse-before-better-stem-cell-therapy/)
explains what is expected and what is not.

## Can you have both?

Some clinics sell combined packages — a joint injection plus an IV "for the whole body". It can be a
reasonable plan for some patients, and the physician is the one to decide. Ask one question before you
agree: **what is each part meant to do for me?** If the answer for the IV part is vague, you are paying
for the vague part too.

## Questions to ask about the route

- Which route do you recommend for my case, and what evidence supports it?
- How many cells will I receive — per joint, or per kilogram — and will that be in writing?
- For an injection: is it ultrasound-guided?
- For an IV: how long is the infusion, and how am I monitored during and after it?
- Which laboratory prepares the cells, and does every batch come with a quality certificate?

## Common questions

### Is IV stem cell therapy good for knee pain?
The knee research mostly uses injection into the joint, and an animal study comparing the two routes
favored the injection. An IV infusion sends most cells to the lungs first, not to the knee. For a painful
joint, ask the physician why IV would be preferred over a local injection in your case. Our article on
[stem cell therapy for arthritis and joint pain](/en/blog/stem-cell-therapy-arthritis-joint-pain/) covers
what the joint research shows.

### How long does a stem cell IV take?
No more than 30 minutes, in a single clinic visit — the drip itself plus the monitoring afterwards.
The clinic confirms your timing in advance so you can plan the day around it.

### Is IV stem cell therapy safe?
In controlled trials pooling thousands of patients, the most common effect linked to IV mesenchymal stem
cells was a temporary fever, with no increase in infection, clots or cancer. That record comes from
tested cells and monitored settings — so the laboratory and the clinic matter. Read more in
[is stem cell therapy safe?](/en/blog/is-stem-cell-therapy-safe/)

### How much does a stem cell IV cost in Cancún?
In our network, the general-wellness IV is dosed at 2 million cells per kilogram and costs $2,300 to
$7,500 depending on body weight — a 70 kg (154 lb) patient receives about 140 million cells. A single
joint injection is $2,300 for 56 million cells. See the full [price list](/en/pricing/).

### Does IV stem cell therapy work for anti-aging?
The evidence for IV cells as a general wellness or anti-aging treatment is limited, and results vary from
person to person. It is considered investigational. A good physician will tell you that plainly before
you decide.
