## Short answer

Plan on paying for it yourself. U.S. insurers generally classify mesenchymal stem cell injections for
joints and other orthopedic uses as investigational, and decline them — Blue Cross Blue Shield's
policy for federal employees, for one, says so for every orthopedic application. Original Medicare
generally does not pay for care outside the U.S. at all. In Canada, Ontario funds out-of-country care
only with written approval in advance and only for treatment that is not experimental, and other
provinces have rules of their own. None of this changes if the clinic is in your own city rather than
in Mexico.

There are a few things worth checking before you write the whole trip off as uninsurable: the tests
your own doctor orders at home, how a health-spending account or a tax credit treats the invoice, and
what a travel policy says about planned procedures. Each is covered below, with the official page it
comes from.

Regenerative cell therapies are considered investigational and are not approved by the U.S. FDA or
Health Canada for these uses. Results vary from person to person.

## Why insurers say no

Insurers pay for treatments that are approved or accepted as standard care for a specific condition.
Stem cell injections for arthritis, tendon injuries or general wellness have not reached that point.
The trials are small and the protocols differ, which is why insurers file them under "experimental or
investigational." That label is what the denial letter will cite.

You may have read that insurance *does* cover stem cell transplants. It does, and that is a different
procedure. A bone marrow or stem cell transplant for blood cancers such as leukemia, lymphoma or
myeloma rebuilds the blood and immune system; Medicare has a national coverage policy for it. A
regenerative injection into a knee or a vein is not the same treatment, and coverage for one tells you
nothing about the other.

### Medicare

Original Medicare generally does not pay for care received outside the United States; the exceptions
are narrow emergency situations. Most Medigap plans cover emergency care abroad, at 80% after a $250
deductible and up to a $50,000 lifetime limit. Medicare Advantage plans follow Medicare's rules for
care outside the U.S., though your plan may cover more — check with it before you travel. The Medigap
benefit is for emergencies, not for treatment you planned the trip around.

### HSAs and FSAs

This is the question people most often hope has a yes. The honest answer is that it depends, and it is
not ours to give. The IRS sets out what counts as a medical expense in Publication 502, and two of its
rules apply here:

- **Cosmetic procedures are generally excluded.** So skin and facial treatments are unlikely to
  qualify.
- **Drugs used abroad have their own rule.** A prescribed drug you buy and use in another country
  counts only if it is legal both there and in the United States. A cell product the FDA has not
  approved sits awkwardly under that rule.

One more detail matters: you pay Kern Stem Care, a coordination agency, and your invoice separates the
medical portion from the rest. Show it to your plan administrator or tax preparer before you pay, not
after.

### Canada

Ontario's health plan funds out-of-country treatment only if it is approved in writing beforehand, and
only if the service is not experimental or for research. Travel, accommodation and meals are not
covered either. Other provinces and territories set their own
rules, so check yours. The federal Medical Expense Tax Credit is broader: the Canada Revenue Agency
says you can generally claim eligible amounts even if they were not paid in Canada. Procedures done
solely for cosmetic reasons are not eligible. Because your
payment goes to an agency that then pays the provider, ask your accountant how the itemized invoice
should be claimed.

### Travel insurance

Canada's official travel advice says most travel insurance policies won't cover planned medical
procedures in another country, and that a provincial plan may not cover complications that develop in
the country where you had the procedure. If you are buying cover in the U.S., check the same two
points. Read the exclusions rather than the brochure, and ask the insurer directly, in writing, whether a planned
regenerative injection in Mexico — and its complications — is covered.

## FAQ

### Does Blue Cross Blue Shield cover stem cell therapy?
Not for orthopedic uses such as knee or back injections, as a rule. Blue Cross Blue Shield is a group
of separate companies, each with its own policies, but the federal employee program's policy and those
of several state plans list mesenchymal stem cell therapy as investigational for orthopedic
applications. Check your own plan's medical policy or call the number on your card.

### Will my insurance cover stem cell therapy for knees?
Generally not. Knee injections are the most studied use, but the evidence is still short of what
insurers require for standard care. What the research does and does not show is in our post on
[knee cartilage](/en/blog/can-stem-cells-repair-knee-cartilage/).

### Can I get reimbursed later if I keep the receipts?
Possibly, but don't count on it. Submitting an itemized invoice costs nothing, and some patients do it.
Ask first whether the plan will even consider the claim, so the decision to travel doesn't rest on a
reimbursement that may never come.

### If something goes wrong after I'm home, will my insurance pay for it?
We can't answer that for your plan, and nobody else can either except your insurer. Whether a plan
pays for complications of a treatment it did not cover is set by its own terms, and Canada's travel
advice warns that a provincial plan may not pay for complications that develop abroad. Ask your insurer before you go, and get the answer
in writing.

candidate and you have the written quote.
