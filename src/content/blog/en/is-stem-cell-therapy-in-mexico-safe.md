## Short answer

Stem cell therapy in Mexico can be done safely: across 55 randomized trials with 2,696 patients,
the only side effect consistently linked to mesenchymal stem cells was a temporary fever. The
country is not what makes it safe or unsafe. The specific clinic, the laboratory that prepared the
cells, and how the trip is planned around the procedure are.

That is a narrower answer than either side of the argument likes. Clinics selling the treatment
tend to say "completely safe". Headlines about "stem cell tourism" tend to say "dangerous". The
evidence supports neither. This page covers the parts of the question that are specific to
Mexico — what the regulator actually checks, how it compares with the U.S., and the risks that
come from travelling. For the general side effects of the therapy itself, see
[Is stem cell therapy safe?](/en/blog/is-stem-cell-therapy-safe/)

## What the safety data says

The best summary of the evidence is a 2020 systematic review from the Ottawa Hospital Research
Institute, which pooled 55 randomized controlled trials of mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) given
intravenously to 2,696 adults. Compared with patients who did not receive the cells:

- **Fever** was more common. It was the only consistent difference, and it was temporary.
- **Infection, blood clots, cancer and death** were not more common.
- **No trial was stopped early** because of a safety problem.

Two honest limits. These were controlled trials, run with well-characterised cells under
research conditions — the review does not tell you how a given clinic performs. And "safe" is
not the same as "effective": these are investigational therapies, and the review does not show
they work for any particular condition.

## So where does the risk actually come from?

When something has gone badly wrong, the product and the provider are what failed, not the
cell type. The clearest documented case happened in the United States. In 2018 the CDC reported
that a dozen patients in three states were hospitalised with serious bacterial infections after
receiving umbilical cord blood products that were contaminated before they ever reached the
clinic.

That is the pattern worth remembering. The risks that matter are:

| Risk | Where it comes from | What to check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Infection | A contaminated batch, or poor sterile technique | A certificate of analysis for your batch, with sterility testing |
| Wrong or too few cells | No verified cell count or viability | Flow cytometry results on that certificate |
| Treatment you did not need | No real medical evaluation before you pay | Who evaluates you, and whether that person can say no |
| Complications after you leave | No plan for follow-up at home | Who you call, and who answers, once you are back |

## What Mexico's regulator checks — and what it says

Mexico's federal health authority, COFEPRIS, licenses the clinics and laboratories that work with
cells. It also enforces those licences. In 2015 it shut down the cell-therapy area of a Mexico City
clinic, and the reasons it gave are a useful checklist:

- no research protocols supporting the treatment being offered;
- no clinical studies in humans backing it;
- no flow cytometry to confirm the cell count and viability of what was being injected.

In the same notice, COFEPRIS warned patients that stem cell treatments lack authorisation because
their efficacy is not proven. We agree, and say the same on every page: a government licence
(COFEPRIS) means the establishment meets Mexico's requirements to operate. It does not mean the
treatment is approved for a condition — in Mexico, in the U.S. by the FDA, or in Canada by Health
Canada.

How to read and check a licence is explained in

## Is it safer in the U.S.?

Not automatically. The U.S. has its own unlicensed and poorly run stem cell clinics — the 2018
infections above happened there. The real difference is in what each country allows:

- **In the U.S.**, clinics without FDA approval may generally offer only minimally processed cells,
  for a narrow set of uses. Cells grown in a lab to larger numbers ("expanded") need FDA approval or a
  clinical trial.
- **In Mexico**, licensed laboratories may prepare expanded cells, including from donated
  umbilical cord tissue.

More cells means the laboratory matters more, not less. That is why the batch certificate in the
table above is the single most useful document you can ask for.

## The risks that come from the trip

These are specific to travelling for treatment, and they are the easiest to plan for:

- **The flight home.** Long flights and sitting still raise the risk of blood clots for anyone.
  Ask the treating physician how soon you can fly after your procedure, and plan to walk and drink
  water on the way.
- **Follow-up once you are home.** Know before you leave who you contact if you get a fever,
  swelling that grows instead of settling, or pain that worsens after 48 hours — and confirm that
  someone will answer.
- **Your records.** Bring home a written summary of what you received: cell type, dose, batch
  number, and the name of the physician. Your own doctor will need it.
- **Cover.** Your health plan may not pay for treatment abroad, or for complications from it. See
  [Does insurance cover stem cell therapy?](/en/blog/does-insurance-cover-stem-cell-therapy/)
- **If something goes wrong.** Mexico has a national medical arbitration commission, CONAMED,
  which handles complaints about medical care, including private care.

## FAQ

### Is stem cell therapy in Mexico FDA-approved?
No. The FDA regulates treatment given in the U.S., and it has not approved any stem cell therapy for
orthopedic, aesthetic or wellness use. Health Canada has not either. A government licence (COFEPRIS)
covers the establishment, not the treatment's effectiveness.

### What are the most common side effects?
Soreness, swelling or bruising where the cells were injected, and sometimes a mild fever or tiredness
for a day or two. A fever that climbs, or swelling and pain that get worse after 48 hours, are not
typical and should be reported to the physician right away.

### Is Cancún itself safe to travel to for treatment?
Millions of international visitors travel to Cancún every year. Check your government's current
travel advisory before you book, as you would for any trip. If you would rather not navigate the city
on treatment day, airport and clinic transfers are available as an optional service.

### Can I see the certificate for my cell batch before treatment?
You should, and a legitimate laboratory will show it to you. If a provider cannot tell you where the
cells came from and how they were tested, that is your answer.
