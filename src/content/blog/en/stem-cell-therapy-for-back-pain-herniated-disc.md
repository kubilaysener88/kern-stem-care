## Short answer

So far, stem cell injections into a spinal disc show modest benefit at best: the largest trial, with
404 patients, missed its main goal against a saline injection. Nearly all of that research is about
worn discs that hurt. For a herniated disc pressing on a nerve we did not find a controlled trial of
cells, and many herniations shrink on their own over time.

This treatment is considered investigational and is not approved by the U.S. FDA or Health Canada for
back pain. Our published price list does not include the spine, so we are not giving you a figure
here. Below is what we can tell you.

## First: when not to wait

If back pain comes with numbness around your buttocks or genitals, loss of feeling in one or both legs,
being unable to urinate, or losing control of your bladder or bowels, go to an emergency room now and do
not drive yourself. Those are the warning signs published by the [UK's National Health
Service](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/slipped-disc/), and no regenerative treatment replaces that visit.

## "Back pain" is several different problems

What changes the answer most is where your pain comes from:

| What you have | What usually happens | Where the cell research stands |
|---|---|---|
| **A worn disc that hurts** (discogenic pain, mostly lower back) | Can last months or years | This is what has been studied: trials injecting into the disc |
| **A herniated disc with pain down the leg** | Usually improves slowly with gentle activity and painkillers | Some small series recorded protrusion size; we found no controlled trials |
| **Arthritis of the spine's joints, or a narrow spinal canal** | Depends on the case | The trials here do not apply: they studied the disc |

On herniation, one figure is worth having before you spend anything. A review of studies with repeat
MRI scans in patients who did not have surgery estimated that the herniation regressed on its own in
about 13% of bulging discs, 53% of protrusions, 70% of extrusions and 93% of sequestered fragments
([Rashed et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.3171/2023.6.SPINE23367)). The NHS says a slipped disc
usually gets better slowly with rest, gentle exercise and painkillers, and that surgery is not usually
needed.

## What the trials show in worn discs

These are the most useful studies with a comparison group:

| Study | Patients | Compared with | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| [Noriega et al., 2017](https://doi.org/10.1097/TP.0000000000001484) | 24 | Sham injection | Quick improvement, apparently limited to a group of responders: about 40% |
| [RESPINE, Pers et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1136/ard-2024-225771) | 114 | Sham injection | Missed its goal: 74% responded with cells, 69% with placebo |
| [Beall et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.spinee.2025.03.015) | 404 | Saline in the disc | Missed its primary and secondary goals; cells with hyaluronic acid did lower average pain at 12 and 24 months |

Three things help you read that table:

**The placebo effect here is strong.** People who did not get cells improved too, sometimes almost as
much. That is why a testimonial, or an improvement in a study with no comparison group, says far less
than it seems to.

**The overall picture is modest.** A meta-analysis of seven trials found an advantage for cells of
about 4 points on a 100-point disability scale, and its authors call that modest
([Rahyussalim et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.31616/asj.2025.0354)). For context, another review
treats 10 points as the smallest change a patient notices, and concludes that controlled studies often
failed to show a marked difference ([Vadalà et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.14245/ns.2551046.523)).

**A result does not transfer to every product.** The 404-patient trial used one specific product from
the company Mesoblast, which also funded it. The company highlights the pain reduction at 12 months; the
published paper is clear that the prespecified primary goal was not reached. A second trial of 300
patients is under way, and [the company expects results in mid-2027](https://www.biospace.com/press-releases/mesoblast-achieves-target-of-300-treated-patients-in-pivotal-phase-3-trial-for-chronic-low-back-pain).
Those data describe that product, not the cells at any given clinic.

One signal is worth raising with your physician: in that same trial, results were better in people who
had been in pain for less than 68 months. It is a subgroup analysis, so it points somewhere without
proving anything.

## Do they regrow the disc?

The evidence does not let anyone say so. In some studies, MRI showed more hydration or a stable disc,
but only sporadically. In the 404-patient trial there was no difference between groups on the disc-wear
scale, and a similar share needed another procedure at that level afterwards. So if someone promises you
"a new disc" or a way out of surgery, ask for the study behind it.

## The risks of putting a needle in a disc

In the trials the injection was well tolerated: the large trial and RESPINE reported no serious events
related to the treatment. A review of 16 studies and 607 people recorded one case of disc infection
([Journal of Pain Research, 2022](https://doi.org/10.2147/JPR.S373345)). Even so, three points are worth
knowing:

- **The puncture itself.** A 10-year study compared discs punctured for a diagnostic test (discography)
  with discs left alone: degeneration progressed in 35% versus 14%, with 55 new herniations versus 22
  ([Carragee et al., 2009](https://doi.org/10.1097/BRS.0b013e3181ab5432)). That was a diagnostic test in
  people without serious back pain, not a cell treatment, and in the 404-patient trial loss of disc height
  was uncommon. It is a reason not to puncture a disc without a clear indication.
- **The product.** The infection cluster documented by the CDC included infections around the spine, in
  patients treated mostly for joint or back pain. We cover it in [Is stem cell therapy in Mexico
  safe?](/en/blog/is-stem-cell-therapy-in-mexico-safe/). What protects you is the certificate of analysis
  for your batch, with sterility testing.
- **The exact location.** Inside the disc is not the same as inside the fluid around the spinal cord. *The
  New England Journal of Medicine* described a proliferative lesion of the spinal cord as a complication of
  spinal infusions abroad ([Berkowitz et al., 2016](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMc1600188)). Always ask where
  exactly the needle goes.

Safety data beyond two years are still scarce.

## Before you accept a spine treatment, ask

1. Which disc is causing my pain, and how was that confirmed?
2. Where exactly does the needle go, who places it, and with what imaging guidance?
3. Which cells are these, from which laboratory, and can I see the certificate for my batch?
4. Which study with a comparison group supports what you are proposing?
5. If I get a fever or more pain once I am home, who do I call?

## FAQ

### Do IV stem cells work for a herniated disc or back pain?
The trials here injected cells into the disc, so their results do not describe an IV infusion. Given by vein,
cells pass through the lungs first, as we explain in [IV vs. joint injection](/en/blog/stem-cell-iv-vs-joint-injection/).

### Who was in these studies?
Mostly adults with chronic low back pain from disc wear that had not improved with physical therapy
and other conservative care. In [one trial](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.spinee.2020.10.004), for example, they had at least 6 months of pain and had tried 3
months of conservative treatment. If your case is different, these results tell you less.

### How long would any effect take?
The trials measured results at 6, 12 and 24 months, not in days. What to expect over time is in
[the results timeline](/en/blog/stem-cell-therapy-results-timeline/).

### Does insurance cover it?
For orthopedic uses, as a rule, no. The details are in
[Does insurance cover stem cell therapy?](/en/blog/does-insurance-cover-stem-cell-therapy/).

### Should I stop my current treatment?
Not without talking to your doctor. These studies enrolled people for whom conservative care had not been
enough; they did not compare cells with stopping it.
