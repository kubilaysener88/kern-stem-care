## Short answer

"Cell therapy center" sounds like one place. It rarely is. Behind a stem cell treatment there are
usually **three separate parties**: a **laboratory** that sources, processes and tests the cells; a
**clinic** where they are applied; and a **treating physician** who decides whether the therapy fits
your case and administers it. Each holds its own licence and carries its own responsibility.

Knowing which is which lets you ask the right question of the right person. This article follows the
cells from the tissue they come from to the room where you receive them.

## First: two very different things share the name

Search for "cell therapy center" and two worlds come back mixed together:

- **Hospital bone marrow transplant centers.** They treat blood cancers with *hematopoietic*
  (blood-forming) stem cells. It involves chemotherapy and a hospital stay, and it lives in large
  hospitals.
- **Regenerative medicine centers.** They work with *mesenchymal* stem cells (MSCs) for joints,
  orthopedics, skin and wellness. It is an outpatient procedure — an injection or an IV.

This article is about the second kind, which is what we coordinate. If you need a bone marrow
transplant for a blood cancer, the route is your hematologist and a transplant hospital.

## Step 1: where the cells come from

Mesenchymal stem cells are taken from tissues that naturally contain them. The most common sources:

| Source | How it is obtained | Your own or a donor's |
|---|---|---|
| Umbilical cord tissue | From a cord donated after a birth, with the mother's consent | Donor (allogeneic) |
| Adipose tissue (fat) | A small liposuction on the patient | Usually your own (autologous) |
| Bone marrow | A needle draw, usually from the hip | Your own or a donor's |

**Autologous** means the cells come from your body: the risk of an immune reaction is lower, but you
need a harvesting procedure first, and how many cells you get — and how well they behave — depends on
your age and health. **Allogeneic** means they come from a screened donor: no harvesting procedure for
you, and the batch can be tested before you arrive.

**What to ask:** which tissue do *my* cells come from, and are they mine or a donor's? If a donor's,
how was that donor screened?

## Step 2: what the laboratory does with them

This is where most of what you never see gets decided. Broadly, a cell therapy laboratory:

1. **Isolates** the mesenchymal cells from the tissue.
2. **Grows and multiplies them.** A tissue sample holds relatively few cells; reaching tens of
   millions means expanding them in culture. Each round of growth is called a *passage*. Cells kept
   in culture too long can age and lose capacity, so the passage number is worth knowing.
3. **Confirms they are what they claim to be.** In 2006 the International Society for Cellular
   Therapy (ISCT) set minimum criteria: the cells carry certain surface markers (CD73, CD90, CD105),
   lack markers typical of blood cells (such as CD34, CD45 and HLA-DR), and can be turned into bone,
   cartilage and fat in the lab. The test that reads the markers is called *flow cytometry*.
4. **Confirms they are safe.** Sterility testing (bacteria, fungi and mycoplasma), infectious-disease
   screening of the donor, and genetic-stability testing of the cells.
5. **Counts the living cells.** The *count* says how many there are; *viability* says what share is
   alive. Both matter — a dose of dead cells is not the dose.
6. **Stores and ships them.** Depending on the product, cells travel frozen or chilled, and arrive at
   the clinic with their paperwork.

All of that ends in one document: the **batch certificate**. It records what was done to *your* batch
and what the results were.

**What to ask:** can I see the certificate for my batch, with count, viability, identity and
sterility? Who signs it?

## Step 3: where, and by whom, they are applied

The **clinic** is the physical facility. It needs its own government licence (COFEPRIS) for the
activity it performs, in that company's name and at that address. The **treating physician** is the
person who reviews your history, decides whether the therapy makes sense for you, administers it —
into a joint, sometimes ultrasound-guided, or by IV — and follows up afterwards.

The physician is also the one who can tell you no. A serious evaluation is one that is allowed to end
in "this isn't right for you."

**What to ask:** what is the name of the physician who will treat me, and their *cédula profesional*
(Mexican medical licence) number? Do they evaluate me before I pay and travel?

licence and each *cédula* in the official registers, and

## FAQ

### Where is stem cell therapy given in Mexico?
At regenerative medicine clinics holding a government licence (COFEPRIS), by a physician with a
*cédula profesional*. There are clinics in several cities — Cancún, Tijuana, Guadalajara, Mexico City,
Monterrey. What changes from one to the next is the clinic, the lab that supplies it, and the
physician. If you are comparing destinations, see
[cost in Cancún vs. Tijuana vs. the U.S.](/en/blog/stem-cell-therapy-cost-cancun-tijuana-usa/).

### Are my own cells better than donor cells?
There is no single answer. Your own cells lower the risk of an immune reaction, but need an extra
procedure and depend on your age and health. Donor cells come from screened tissue and skip that step.
Which suits your case is the treating physician's call.

### How long do the cells stay in the body?
Research suggests most applied mesenchymal cells do not stay long — days to weeks — and that much of
their effect comes from the signals they release while they are there, rather than from settling in
to build new tissue. That is why changes, when they come, tend to take weeks or months; see
[how long it takes to see results](/en/blog/stem-cell-therapy-results-timeline/).

### Is getting stem cell injections a good idea?
It depends on your case — which is exactly the question the treating physician answers at your
evaluation. Many regenerative therapies are considered investigational and are **not approved by the
U.S. FDA or Health Canada** for specific conditions. Results vary from person to person and none are
guaranteed. The real risks are laid out in
[is stem cell therapy safe?](/en/blog/is-stem-cell-therapy-safe/).

### Can I ask to see my batch certificate before treatment?
Yes, and you should. It is a routine document at a serious lab, and it should be no trouble to show
you.
