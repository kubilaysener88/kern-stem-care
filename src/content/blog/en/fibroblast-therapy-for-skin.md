## Short answer

**[Fibroblast therapy](/en/services/fibroblasts/)** for skin uses your own fibroblasts — the cells that produce
collagen and build the connective tissue under your skin — grown from a small sample in a lab and injected back
into the treated area. It's studied mainly for wrinkles, acne scars, and facial contour. It is **not the same
procedure** as a "plasma fibroblast" or "Plasma Pen" treatment, a different, device-based technique that many
patients confuse it with online — see the comparison below before you book anything. Fibroblast cell therapy is
not FDA-approved for any specific condition and results vary.

## What the research says

- **Mechanism.** Fibroblasts are the working cells of the dermis: they produce collagen and elastin and rebuild
  connective tissue after injury. Autologous fibroblast therapy cultures a patient's own cells from a small skin
  sample, then re-introduces them into the treated area to add to that collagen-producing population.
- **Reported outcomes.** Clinical studies on autologous cultured fibroblast injections report improvements in
  wrinkles, acne scars, and facial contour defects, generally described as safe with no serious adverse effects
  in the populations studied.
- **Still an evolving field.** Culture technique, number of sessions, and injection protocol vary between
  clinics and researchers, and larger standardized trials are still needed to confirm effect sizes and how long
  results last.

## Fibroblast cell therapy vs. "plasma fibroblast" — not the same thing

Both use the word "fibroblast," which is exactly why patients searching online mix them up. They are different
procedures:

| | Fibroblast cell therapy | "Plasma fibroblast" / Plasma Pen |
|---|---|---|
| What it is | Your own cultured skin cells, injected | A handheld device that uses an electrical arc to create controlled micro-injuries |
| How it works | Adds collagen-producing cells to the dermis | Triggers the skin's own wound-healing and contraction response |
| Studied for | Wrinkles, acne scars, facial contour | Skin tightening, especially around the eyelids |
| What Kern Stem Care coordinates | This — cell-based therapy through our partner network | Not this; it's a different, non-cellular procedure we don't coordinate |

If a provider offers you a "fibroblast" treatment, ask directly which one it is. Do this before you book: online
reports of burns and scarring are almost always tied to an unlicensed, device-based "plasma fibroblast"
treatment — including people attempting it on themselves at home — not to clinic-administered cell therapy.

## Fibroblasts vs. exosomes vs. stem cells for skin

All three are studied for skin and aesthetic applications, but they're not interchangeable. Fibroblast therapy
adds living, collagen-producing cells; [exosome therapy](/en/blog/exosome-therapy-for-skin/) uses cell-free
signaling vesicles; stem cell therapy uses a different type of living cell entirely. See our
[stem cells vs. exosomes comparison](/en/blog/stem-cells-vs-exosomes-difference/) for that half of the picture.
Only a physician's evaluation can tell you which one — if any — fits your case.

## Common questions

### How long do results last?
Duration varies by individual, protocol, and number of sessions. Ask your treating physician what to expect for
your specific skin and goals — see our general guide on
[results and timelines for cellular therapies](/en/blog/stem-cell-therapy-results-timeline/).

### Is fibroblast therapy FDA-approved?
No — like other regenerative therapies, it is not approved by the U.S. FDA or Health Canada for specific
conditions and is considered investigational. Outcomes vary and no result is guaranteed.

### Can I get a "plasma fibroblast" (Plasma Pen) treatment through Kern Stem Care?
No. We coordinate access to the cell-based fibroblast therapy described above, delivered by licensed physicians
at our partner clinics. A Plasma Pen treatment is a different, device-based procedure and isn't part of what we
coordinate.

### Does it hurt?
Both the sample collection and the injections are minor procedures typically done under local anesthetic; mild
redness or soreness for a day or two is commonly reported. Your physician reviews specific risks with you before
treatment.
