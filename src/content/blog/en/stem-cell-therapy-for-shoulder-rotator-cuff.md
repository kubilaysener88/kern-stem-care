## Short answer

For the shoulder, the strongest stem cell evidence is not for an injection on its own. It is for cells
added *during* rotator cuff surgery: in a 2025 meta-analysis of five comparative studies (415 patients),
the tendon tore again in 17.7% of shoulders that received cells, against 35% with surgery alone. Pain and
function ended up about the same in both groups.

For a stem cell injection *without* surgery — the kind most people are asking about — the evidence is
much thinner: small studies without a comparison group, a few of them encouraging, and no large
randomised trial that we could find.

These therapies are considered investigational and are not approved by the U.S. FDA or Health Canada for
any shoulder condition. Results vary from person to person, and some people notice no improvement.

## Which shoulder problem you have changes the answer

"Shoulder pain" covers several different problems, and the research looks different for each one.

| Your shoulder | What it is | What the stem cell research shows |
|---|---|---|
| **Partial rotator cuff tear** | The tendon is frayed or torn part of the way through | One small Korean trial of an injection into the tendon, with good results and no control group |
| **Full-thickness tear, repaired with surgery** | The tendon is torn through and is reattached to the bone | Cells added during the repair: fewer re-tears, similar pain and function |
| **Shoulder arthritis** | Wear of the cartilage in the ball-and-socket joint | Few studies, done in very different ways; reviews call the evidence limited |
| **Frozen shoulder** | The joint capsule thickens and stiffens | No human trials of stem cells that we could find; only animal studies |

## What the studies actually found

- **Cells added during surgery.** The longest follow-up comes from a hospital near Paris. Surgeons added
  concentrated bone marrow from the patient's own hip to 45 rotator cuff repairs and compared them with 45
  matched repairs without it. At ten years, 87% of the treated tendons were still intact, against 44% of
  the others. It was a case-controlled study, not a randomised trial, and the cells were part of an
  operation. A 2025 meta-analysis that pooled five such studies found the same direction: fewer re-tears,
  but no difference in pain, range of motion or shoulder scores.
- **An injection into the tendon, without surgery.** In Seoul, researchers injected fat-derived stem cells
  from each patient into partially torn rotator cuffs, in about 20 people. They reported no
  treatment-related adverse events. Pain fell sharply, and in the higher-dose groups the tendon defects
  shrank on MRI, with the results holding at two years. It was a first-in-human trial: everyone received cells, so there was no
  comparison group to rule out natural healing or the placebo effect.
- **Shoulder arthritis.** A 2025 systematic review of injections for shoulder arthritis (1,125 patients)
  found the most consistent, if modest, benefit for hyaluronic acid. For stem cells and other
  biologics, it called the evidence limited. A 2026 review added that randomised trials of a
  bone-marrow-cell injection alone in the shoulder are essentially absent.

One detail matters when you compare offers: the Paris and Seoul studies both used the patient's *own*
cells, from bone marrow or fat. Many providers instead use donor cells grown in a laboratory, often from umbilical cord
tissue. Those are a different product, and these results do not automatically carry over to them. Ask
which kind you would receive. Our post on [what a cell therapy center does](/en/blog/what-is-a-cell-therapy-center/)
explains the difference.

## What this means if you are considering treatment

A rotator cuff torn all the way through, especially after an injury with sudden weakness, should be seen
by an orthopedic surgeon at home first. Some tears heal best when they are repaired early, and no
injection has been shown to reattach a tendon to the bone.

For a partial tear, tendon wear or mild to moderate arthritis that has not improved with physical therapy,
a physician may consider a stem cell injection a reasonable option to try for pain and function. It has
not been shown to help you avoid surgery or a shoulder replacement: no trial has tested that.

Before your evaluation, gather:

- **An MRI or ultrasound** of the shoulder, which shows whether the tendon is torn and how far.
- **An X-ray**, if arthritis is suspected, which shows how much joint space is left.
- **What you have already tried**: physical therapy, cortisone or other injections, and how each went.

And ask the treating physician:

- Is my tendon partially torn, fully torn, or worn? Would surgery do more for it?
- Will the injection be guided by ultrasound, and into the joint or into the tendon?
- What would count as a good result for my shoulder, and by when?

The joint is not the only thing that varies. Where the cells go — into the joint, or into a vein — also
changes what the research says; see [IV stem cell therapy vs. joint injection](/en/blog/stem-cell-iv-vs-joint-injection/).

## FAQ

### Can stem cells heal a torn rotator cuff without surgery?
For a partial tear, one small trial without a control group reported smaller defects on MRI after an
injection into the tendon. For a tendon torn all the way through, there is no good evidence that an
injection alone reattaches it. The cell results for full tears come from studies in which the tendon was
also repaired surgically.

### Can a stem cell injection help me avoid a shoulder replacement?
No trial has shown that. Some people use injections to manage pain while they put off surgery, but
whether that delays a replacement has not been established. Be wary of any provider who promises it.

### Do stem cells help a frozen shoulder?
We found no human trials of stem cells for frozen shoulder, only laboratory studies in animals. Physical
therapy, cortisone injections and, in some trials, platelet-rich plasma (PRP) have been studied far more.

### Is a shoulder stem cell injection painful?
Expect soreness at the injection site, and sometimes a temporary flare of pain, stiffness or swelling in
the first days that usually eases within one to two weeks. Our post on
[pain getting worse before it gets better](/en/blog/pain-worse-before-better-stem-cell-therapy/) covers
what is expected and what is not.

### How long until I know whether it worked?
In the Korean tendon trial, the improvement was already measured at one year and held at two. Patient
reports generally place any change between a few weeks and about six months. See
[how long results take to appear](/en/blog/stem-cell-therapy-results-timeline/).

### Is it approved by the FDA?
No. Stem cell injections for rotator cuff tears or shoulder arthritis are not approved by the FDA or
Health Canada and are considered investigational.
